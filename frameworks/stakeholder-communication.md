# Framework: Stakeholder Communication

**Purpose:** How Steve communicates with different audiences — what they care about, what to lead with, how to adapt his style without losing authenticity.

---

## The Core Principle

Communication is not about expressing yourself clearly. It's about being received clearly by the other person. The same message that lands perfectly with a sales rep will miss with a CFO. Steve's job is to translate his thinking into the language of whoever he's talking to.

This is not manipulation — it's professional respect. Meeting people in their world is how effective leaders operate.

---

## Communication by Audience

---

### Your Boss

**What they care about:**
- No surprises on numbers or team issues
- That Steve is making their life easier, not harder
- That Steve is developing — they want to advocate for him and need evidence
- The team is performing and issues are being handled without needing to escalate
- Steve's forecast is accurate (they have to commit their forecast upward based on his)

**What to lead with:**
- Business outcome first, context second
- "We're at [X]% of quota with [X] days left in the quarter. Here's my plan for the gap."
- Not: "I've been working really hard on the team and here's everything that's happened..."

**Format:**
- Brief, structured: headline, then supporting points
- Weekly: 3-bullet status email is sufficient (wins, risks, needs)
- 1:1 meetings: own the agenda, don't just respond

**Tone:** Direct, confident, honest about problems. Don't minimize issues or inflate wins — your boss will find out either way.

**When to escalate:** Bring problems early with a proposed solution. "Here's an issue, here's what I'm planning to do, is that right?" — not "I have a problem, what should I do?" and not (worst case) silence until it explodes.

**Email subject line pattern:** [URGENT] / [FYI] / [DECISION NEEDED] — always signal what action the email requires.

**Template — Weekly status to boss:**
```
Subject: Weekly Status — [Team Name] — [Date]

Wins this week:
- [Specific win with number]
- [Specific win]

Watch items:
- [Issue or risk] — [What I'm doing about it]
- [Issue or risk] — [What I'm doing about it]

Needs from you:
- [Specific ask, if any]
- [Nothing this week]

Forecast: [Quarter-to-date] / [Full quarter target] — [Confidence level]
```

### Executive Team (VP, C-Suite)

**What they care about:**
- Revenue, margin, growth rate, risk
- Is the sales organization a competitive advantage or a constraint?
- Are they getting accurate information (vs. filtered, optimistic reporting)?
- Does Steve see the business the way they see it?
- Can they trust him at the next level?

**What to lead with:**
- Business impact in dollar terms, always
- "The team generated $2.1M in new pipeline this quarter — here's what's driving it and what the risk is."
- Not: "The team has been doing a lot of great work and here are the highlights..."

**Format:**
- One-page max for written communication
- 3-5 minutes max for verbal update in a meeting you're attending
- Charts/data over words when possible — make it scannable
- Bottom line up front, every time

**Tone:** Confident and direct. Don't over-hedge. Don't perform humility. They respect people who say "Here's what I know, here's what I don't know, here's what I'm going to do."

**How to handle a question you can't answer:** "I don't have that number with me — I'll send it by end of day." Then send it by end of day. Never guess in front of executives.

**How to handle bad news:** Get there first. "I want to flag an issue before it surprises you." Then: what happened, what you're doing, what you need (if anything).

**Email to exec — template:**
```
Subject: [One line that says what this is about and what action is needed]

Bottom line: [One or two sentences — what is the situation and what is Steve doing or recommending?]

Context:
- [Bullet 1]
- [Bullet 2]
- [Bullet 3]

What I need from you (if anything): [Specific ask or "No action needed — informational only"]
```

### Peer Managers (Other Sales Managers, Cross-Functional Peers)

**What they care about:**
- Collaboration that actually helps them (not just Steve)
- Not being blamed for things that go wrong
- Getting credit for contributions
- Their team's success and their own career development

**What to lead with:**
- "Here's how this helps both of us" or "I want to get your perspective on..."
- Contribution first, ask second — build credit before drawing on it

**Format:** Conversational is fine. Slack or email depending on urgency. Written for anything that needs a record.

**Tone:** Peer-to-peer, collaborative. Not competitive. Acknowledge their expertise in their domain.

**Collaboration principle:** Be specific about what you need and what you're offering. Vague requests from peers get vague responses. "I'm working on X and could use your input on Y — it would take 20 minutes and I think it'd be useful for you too because Z."

**When there's conflict:** Go directly to them, not around them. "I want to talk about what happened on [X] — I think we see it differently and I'd rather figure it out between us than let it fester."

### Your Direct Reports (Sales Reps)

**What they care about:**
- Am I going to hit my number?
- Does my manager have my back?
- Am I growing? Is this job making me better?
- Am I being treated fairly?
- Do I know where I stand?

**What to lead with:**
- What matters to them about their own results and development
- Not what matters to Steve about the team's results

**Format:**
- 1:1s: conversational, rep-led first, Steve-led second
- Coaching feedback: specific, behavioral, example-based (not vague evaluations)
- Performance feedback: in writing, never as a surprise in a formal review

**Tone:** Direct but caring. Steve can tell a rep hard truths about their performance because he genuinely wants them to succeed. The hard truth said with care lands differently than the same truth said with frustration.

**Feedback formula for reps:**
```
Situation: "When [specific situation]..."
Behavior: "...I noticed [specific observable behavior]..."
Impact: "...and the effect was [specific result or consequence]."
Request: "Going forward, I'd like you to [specific alternative behavior]."
```

**Recognition formula:**
```
"I want to call out what [Rep] did on [Specific situation] — they [Specific behavior] and the result was [Specific outcome]. That's exactly the kind of [skill/attitude/approach] that makes us better."
```

Specific > Generic. "Great job!" is forgettable. "The way you handled the objection in that call — you didn't cave, you leaned in with a question — that's what good reps do" is remembered.

### Key Customers

**What they care about:**
- Does my vendor understand my business?
- Will they be there when something goes wrong?
- Am I getting value, not just a product?
- Is this relationship worth my time?

**What to lead with:**
- Their problem, their context, their goals — not Steve's product or offering
- "I've been following what's happening in [construction segment] — wanted to share something relevant and check in on [their situation]"

**Format:** Varies by relationship. Long-term customers: conversational, relationship-first. New or expanding relationships: structured, value-focused.

**Tone:** Consultative, not transactional. Steve's customers should feel like they're talking to someone who understands their world, not someone trying to close them.

**The value-first outreach:**
Before any sales conversation, find a way to give something: relevant market intel, a referral, an insight, a connection. Then the conversation has a different starting energy.

**Difficult customer conversations:**
- Own the problem first: "This didn't go the way it should have. I own that."
- Then the plan: "Here's what we're going to do."
- Then the ask (if there is one): "What I need from you to make this right is..."

---

### Prospects

**What they care about:**
- Do you understand my problem, or are you just pitching me?
- Is this worth my time?
- What's the risk of buying? What's the risk of not buying?
- Can I trust this vendor to deliver?

**What to lead with:**
- Their problem (as Steve understands it, validated by research)
- Not: features, pricing, or company overview

**Discovery-first principle:** Before presenting anything, earn the right by asking questions that show you understand their world. "I've been working with contractors in [segment] and I see a few common patterns — does this resonate with what you're experiencing?"

**First outreach template:**
```
Subject: [Something specific to them — not generic]

[One line about why you're reaching out that shows you did your homework]

[One specific insight or question about their situation]

[Specific ask — small and easy to say yes to: "Would a 20-minute conversation be worth it to see if there's a fit?"]
```

No: "I wanted to reach out and introduce myself and tell you about [Company]..." — generic, deleted.

## Meeting Communication Style

**Walking into any meeting, Steve should know:**
1. What is the purpose of this meeting? (Decision / Update / Brainstorm / Relationship)
2. What does success look like when we walk out?
3. What is his role? (Presenter / Participant / Decision-maker / Observer)

**If facilitating:**
- Start with the purpose and outcome
- Keep it on track — protect the agenda
- End with clear next steps and owners
- Send a recap within 24 hours for anything that requires action

**If participating:**
- Prepare one sharp contribution (insight, question, or perspective) in advance
- Don't talk just to be seen talking — quality over volume
- Ask the question others won't: "What's the risk if this doesn't work?"

## Difficult Conversations Framework

For any conversation that feels hard to have:

**Before the conversation:**
- Get clear on the purpose: What outcome do I need? What do I need them to understand?
- Separate observation from interpretation: What did I see vs. what did I conclude?
- Check your intent: Is this for their benefit, the team's benefit, or for me to vent?

**During the conversation:**
1. Open with purpose: "I want to talk about [X]. My goal is [Y]."
2. Share what you observed: "What I noticed was [specific, factual]."
3. Ask before concluding: "Help me understand what was happening from your side."
4. State the impact: "The effect of this was [specific]."
5. Request a change: "What I need going forward is [specific]."
6. Get a commitment: "What will you do differently?"

**After the conversation:**
- Follow up in writing if a commitment was made
- Check in at an agreed time
- Acknowledge progress when you see it

## When to Escalate vs. Handle Directly

**Handle directly:**
- Misunderstandings
- Process or ownership conflicts
- Disagreements about approach or priority
- Underperformance within your direct team
- Peer who missed a commitment once

**Escalate to your boss:**
- Pattern of behavior that direct conversation hasn't changed
- Conflict with another manager that's affecting business results
- Situation where you need air cover or resources you don't have authority to provide
- Anything involving HR concerns (discrimination, harassment, ethical violations)
- When you've tried direct and it's gotten worse

**Rule:** Escalate with a recommended solution, not just a problem. "Here's the situation, here's what I've tried, here's what I think should happen, do you agree?"

*How Steve communicates determines how far his ideas travel, how much people trust him, and ultimately whether he gets the promotion. Communication is a leadership skill, not a soft skill.*
