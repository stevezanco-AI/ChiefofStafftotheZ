## Overview

This domain tracks the health of Steve's most important professional relationships: upward (boss, executives), lateral (peer managers, cross-functional partners), and downward (his direct reports). Strong relationships here are not soft — they are the operating system for getting things done and building a promotion case.

The failure mode to avoid: being excellent at the work while neglecting the relationships. Promotions are made by people. Relationships are the asset.

### Managing Up — How to Use 1:1s

The 1:1 with your boss is one of the highest-leverage hours in Steve's week. Don't waste it on status updates that could be an email.

**Steve's agenda structure for every 1:1:**
1. **Lead with the business** — what's happening in the team and market? No surprises.
2. **One strategic question** — something you want your boss's perspective on, ideally something that shows you're thinking at the next level
3. **Alignment check** — "Is there anything you need from me this week that I should know about?"
4. **Career thread** — once a month minimum: "I want to make sure I'm on track for Director. What do you see as my current gaps?"
5. **Close with a commitment** — both ways. What will Steve do? What, if anything, will his boss do?

### What Your Boss Needs From You
- No surprises on numbers (good or bad)
- Honest forecasts, not optimistic ones
- Problems surfaced early with proposed solutions
- To be able to brag about you to their boss
- Clear signal when you need air cover or resources

### What Steve Needs From His Boss
- Clear criteria for promotion (explicit, not implied)
- Active advocacy in rooms Steve isn't in
- Honest feedback on blind spots
- Executive introductions and visibility opportunities
- To be defended when appropriate

### Boss Relationship Red Flags
- Feedback is vague or delayed
- Steve isn't hearing what's being said in exec meetings
- Boss is shielding Steve from difficult conversations instead of developing him
- Alignment on promotion criteria has never been made explicit

### How Steve Should Show Up with Peers

Peers are not competitors (even when it feels that way). The managers who get promoted are the ones peers want to work with, not the ones who outshine everyone else.

**Peer relationship principles:**
- Proactively share intel that helps them (construction market data, sales approach insights)
- Ask for their help before you need it — build the credit
- Give credit visibly and specifically: "That was [Peer]'s idea and it worked"
- Don't talk negatively about other managers to leadership
- Surface conflicts directly to the person, not through leadership

**Cross-functional peers to invest in:**
- Finance/CFO's team: Steve's headcount and resource asks go through them
- Marketing: Pipeline generation is a shared accountability
- HR Business Partner: They're in every talent decision and promotion conversation
- Operations/Customer Success: Churn prevention and expansion is a team sport

### How to Build Exec Relationships Without Seeming Needy

- Ask their perspective on a topic they care about (not a favor)
- Send a relevant article with a one-line observation (no ask attached)

- Request a 20-minute "getting to know you" meeting once you have some reason to (a project, a question, an intro from your boss)
- Deliver excellent work on anything visible to them — let the work speak first

## Team Health (Steve's Direct Reports)

Steve's team is the most direct expression of his leadership. Their engagement, growth, and results are his results.

### Team Health Indicators

**Leading indicators (watch these first):**
- Are 1:1s happening consistently?
- Are reps bringing problems to Steve proactively?
- Is the team communicating well with each other?
- Are people growing (quota improvement, skill development)?
- Any sudden behavioral changes (withdrawal, drop in energy)?

**Lagging indicators (what you see after the culture has already shifted):**
- Turnover
- Quota misses
- CRM quality dropping
- Customer complaints

### Team Culture Steve is Building

- Accountability without blame: we own our numbers, we fix our misses, we don't point fingers
- Transparent with each other: no sandbagging, no politics
- Development-focused: getting better is the job, not just hitting quota
- Celebrate genuinely: wins matter, recognition is specific
- Hard conversations happen fast: don't let problems age

## Conflict & Tension

How Steve handles conflict is a leadership signal. At Director level, the ability to navigate conflict without escalating it or avoiding it is a core competency.

### Steve's Conflict Principles

1. **Address it early.** Every avoided conflict becomes a bigger conflict later.
2. **Go to the person, not around them.** Talk to the peer, not about them.
3. **Lead with curiosity, not accusation.** "Help me understand..." works better than "Why did you..."
4. **State the impact, not just the behavior.** "When X happened, the effect on my team was Y."
5. **Escalate when needed — but document that you tried direct resolution first.**

### When to Escalate vs. Handle Directly

**Handle directly:**
- Peer who stepped on your team's deal
- Miscommunication about process or ownership
- Difference of opinion on approach
- Someone who didn't deliver what they committed to

**Escalate to boss:**
- Pattern of behavior that direct conversation hasn't resolved
- Conflict that has business consequences beyond the two parties
- Something that requires a policy or process decision
- Any situation involving HR concerns

## Influence Without Authority

At Director and above, most of what you need to accomplish requires people who don't report to you. This is the skill that separates managers from leaders.

**Steve's framework for influencing without authority:**

1. **Understand what they care about.** Before you ask for anything, know what success looks like for them. Frame your ask in terms of their goals, not yours.

2. **Build credit before you need it.** Help them before you need help. Share useful information. Solve a problem for them unprompted.

3. **Make saying yes easy.** Remove the friction. Have the data ready. Have a draft already done. Make your ask specific, not vague.

4. **Use social proof when appropriate.** "Three other managers are already doing this" is more persuasive than "I think this is a good idea."

5. **Follow through perfectly on the small things.** Trust is built in the details. If you said you'd send that email by Thursday, send it by Thursday.
